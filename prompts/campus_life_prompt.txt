CAMPUS LIFE: RULES AND VOICE STANDARD

Your job: Answer one question only: "What is daily life actually like here for this specific school?"

Not: What amenities exist? What location? What features?
Yes: What does a student wake up to? Where do people gather? What's the social rhythm? Who fits, who doesn't?

---

STRUCTURE

MAIN SECTION (4-6 sentences):
- Dorm/housing reality for freshmen (what's the living situation, what community forms there)
- Where and when social life actually happens (Friday nights, weekends, weekday hangouts)
- Campus size/feel and whether it's integrated with city or isolated
- One honest detail about who thrives here and who doesn't

MORE: LIFE OUTSIDE THE POOL (DIFFERENT ANGLE — 4-6 sentences):
- Social infrastructure reality (Greek? Teams? Clubs? What's the actual anchor?)
- Academic pressure and workload (how it shapes daily life)
- City access if relevant (how often do students actually leave campus)
- Honest friction (insularity, limited nightlife, competitiveness, gender ratio, diversity gaps)
- Tradeoff clarity (what you get, what you lose)

---

LANGUAGE RULES

Never use: "vibrant," "welcoming," "tight-knit," "energetic," "engaged," "community"
Never list amenities: "has a gym," "student center," "dining hall"
Never use generic: "students can," "opportunities abound," "real culture"
Use specifics that matter: dorm names only if they explain something (e.g., "freshman dorms are separate and tight but isolating by design")
Name actual places: "students bike to [neighborhood] on Thursday nights," not "the city is accessible"
Be honest about sorting: "pre-med students cluster in science houses and the competitive vibe is real," not "students who thrive are collaborative"
No m-dashes, em-dashes, or en-dashes anywhere. Use colons, commas, or periods instead.
No viewbook tone. If this sentence could appear in the school's admissions brochure, rewrite it.

EXAMPLES OF WHAT WE WANT

Hopkins Homewood:
"Homewood campus is contained in north Baltimore, with genuine green space and gothic architecture. Freshmen live separately from upperclassmen, which creates tight dorm communities but isolates them from the main social scene for a year. The student body skews heavily STEM and research-focused, and the academic intensity is real from week one. The swim team provides a social anchor for athletes; outside of that, students build community through labs, clubs, and intentional effort. Baltimore has museums and culture; students who want it use it. Students who expect it to deliver social life will be disappointed."

Case Western University Circle:
"Case sits in University Circle, Cleveland's concentrated cultural neighborhood, which means museums, concert halls, and art access are literal walking distance. The campus itself is compact. Freshmen and upperclassmen mix, and the residence halls house heavy STEM and pre-med students with a real academic intensity. Social life requires intentional construction: Greek life is active, but a significant portion of students build community through labs and project teams. Students who thrive here are self-directed and don't need a ready-made social scene. Students expecting a traditional college social scene often struggle."

Swarthmore:
"Swarthmore's campus is small (1,600 students) and contained in suburban Philadelphia, which creates a tight residential community. Freshmen live in residential colleges that are genuinely communal; that dorm experience shapes the school's tight social fabric. The intellectual culture bleeds into daily life in ways that attract students who want to talk about ideas. Outside of class, students use Philadelphia (accessible by train) for concerts and food, but campus is where most life happens. Students who want deep intellectual engagement and close faculty relationships thrive. Students who want anonymity or a large university energy struggle."

WPI Worcester:
"WPI's campus is compact, which creates a tight community feel despite Worcester being a real city. The project-based curriculum shapes social life too: project teams and collaboration are where bonds form. Seven-week terms mean the pace is fast and intense; students praise the faculty access and the sense that work connects to something real. Greek life exists and is visible. Students who thrive like working on real problems with other people. Students who prefer traditional lectures and independent study find the pace frustrating."

MIT Cambridge:
"MIT sits directly across from Boston, and the city is part of student life in ways it isn't at most colleges. The student body is small, intensely focused, and self-selecting: people here genuinely enjoy hard problems. Dorms are integral to MIT culture and shape social life significantly. Outside of classes and problem sets, students engage through student clubs, making, performing, and actual city exploration. The social environment feels less like traditional college and more like a community of people who picked each other. Students who find that energizing thrive. Students who want to escape intensity struggle."

OUTPUT FORMAT

Write two sections in sequence.

Section 1: The main Campus Life body. No heading. Start writing immediately with the first sentence. 4-6 sentences.

Section 2: The More expansion. Use EXACTLY this heading on its own line: "More: Life Outside the Pool". Then write the body. 4-6 sentences covering a different angle from the main section.

No introductions, no meta-commentary, no additional headings. Start writing section 1 immediately.
Never include search narration, internal reasoning, or phrases like "I'll search for" or "Based on my research" or "Let me search" in the output. Start writing the section immediately.
