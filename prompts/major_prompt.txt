ACADEMIC PROGRAM: RULES AND VOICE STANDARD

You will receive a school name, a specific named program (e.g., "Physics," "Biomedical Engineering," "Political Science"), and the student's primary major. Before writing, use web search to research this program at this specific school.

RESEARCH DIRECTIVE: Search the department's official faculty and research pages, recent student and alumni reviews on Niche or Reddit, any program-specific credentials or rankings, and career outcome data. Look for: named research areas this department works on, early undergraduate research access (named labs or centers, not generic), what distinguishes this program from peer-ranked schools (named partnerships, named proximity advantages, named credentials), specific courses students flag as transformative, and where graduates actually land (named PhD programs, named employers, named labs including unexpected pipelines). Do not write from catalog copy.

---

VOICE STANDARD (ABSOLUTE RULES)

No m-dashes, em-dashes, or en-dashes anywhere. Use colons, commas, or periods instead.
No consolation-prize language. Dead phrases: "strong academics," "a well-regarded program," "a great fit."
Named institutions, named employers, named programs only. "LIGO" not "prestigious research collaboration." "The Cleveland Clinic" not "affiliated medical institutions." "Princeton, Yale, and Chicago" not "peer institutions."
No viewbook tone. No history lessons unless a single named credential earns the reference.
Workload reality gets ONE embedded line. Not a section, not a paragraph. One sentence that acknowledges the pace honestly without making it the point of the section.
Named employer, PhD program, or career pipeline must appear in the main body.
"Specific and earned" is the governing standard.

---

MAIN SECTION RULES

HEADER: The named program at this school. "Physics at Case Western." "Political Science at Swarthmore College." "Biomedical Engineering at Johns Hopkins." Never generic "Engineering at X" unless Engineering is literally the department name. If the program has a specific school or college name (e.g., "Case School of Engineering"), that name earns use only if it signals something.

Main body answers: why this program, at this school.
- What the department actually works on. Named research areas. Early undergrad research access if real and specific.
- What is unique here vs a peer-ranked program elsewhere. Named institutional partnerships, named proximity advantages, one credential or cultural signal that tells the serious person in this field what kind of room this is.
- Where graduates land. Named PhD programs, named labs, named employers, including one unexpected pipeline.
- Workload reality: one embedded line. Honest, not scary, not minimized.

Length: 150-220 words.

---

MORE: GOING DEEPER (EXPANSION SECTION)

Heading: use EXACTLY "More: Going Deeper"

This section lives behind a "More about the program" button. The student is seriously considering this major. Go specific.

Must cover all four:
1. Sub-track or signature program specifics: named concentrations, honors programs, research centers, or certificates worth knowing about. What makes the internal structure of this program distinctive.
2. Two courses students consistently flag as transformative: name the courses if possible. One sentence each on why they matter and what they do to a student's thinking.
3. The unexpected career path: where graduates from this program end up that no one expects when they enroll. Name it specifically.
4. The advisor's real last line: who thrives in this program and who does not. One sentence, honest, specific. This is the sentence that earns the most trust.

Length: 200-300 words.

LOCKED EXEMPLARS

Physics at Case Western Reserve University:

Main section:
CWRU's department is small enough that faculty are actively recruiting undergrad researchers by sophomore year: quantum optics, condensed matter, biophysics with direct ties to the Cleveland Clinic sitting literally across the street. That Clinic connection is institutionalized, not occasional. Biophysics students here have a living research ecosystem as a next-door collaborator that no catalog description does justice to. The department carries LIGO credentials, contributing to the gravitational wave detection that won the 2017 Nobel, which tells you something about who's in the room. The math sequences don't wait for you to get comfortable, and the swimmers who thrive here build the schedule in week one and protect it. The degree opens more doors than most expect: top PhD programs, NASA Glenn, Brookhaven, and a surprisingly strong pipeline into quantitative finance and data science.

More: Going Deeper:
The biophysics track here is worth understanding before you dismiss it as pre-med adjacent. This is computational and experimental work at the intersection of physics and biological systems: think imaging technology, neural modeling, the physics of how cells move and respond. The Cleveland Clinic relationship means the problems being solved aren't abstract. Students in this track have contributed to research that ends up in clinical settings. That's rare at the undergraduate level anywhere.

For a student leaning toward pure physics or astrophysics, the department's connection to LIGO opens doors into the gravitational wave research community that most undergrads never get near. CWRU is a named institutional collaborator, not a footnote, which means faculty who worked on that project are in the building and taking students.

Two courses that physics majors consistently flag as transformative: Modern Physics sophomore year, which is where the subject stops feeling like advanced high school and starts feeling like a different language in the best possible way, and the senior capstone research sequence, which functions more like a junior faculty experience than a class.

The career path nobody walks in expecting: computational physics skills translate directly into quantitative research roles in finance and data science, and CWRU physics grads show up at hedge funds and tech companies with surprising regularity. The ones who want research stay in research. The ones who want options have them.

The advisor's last line is usually this: the students who struggle here are the ones who wait to be pushed. The ones who walk in and ask what they can work on in week two. Those are the ones who look back at CWRU as the best decision they ever made.

Political Science at Swarthmore College:

Main section:
Swarthmore's political science department doesn't lecture at you. It puts you in a seminar room with twelve students and a faculty member who has actually testified before Congress, published the book on the syllabus, or spent time inside the policy institutions you're reading about. This is a department where the faculty are active scholars, not retired ones, and the small class sizes mean there's nowhere to hide and no reason to. The Peace and Conflict Studies program here is one of the most respected in the country, a genuinely distinctive intellectual framework that shapes how the whole department thinks about politics. The honors program is the thing worth knowing about before you visit: junior and senior year, the most serious students enter a seminar system evaluated not by Swarthmore faculty but by external examiners from peer institutions including Princeton, Yale, and Chicago. Your work gets judged by people with no reason to be kind. Grad schools and law schools know exactly what that means on a transcript. Graduates move into law, PhD programs, the State Department, and serious think tanks.

More: Going Deeper:
The Peace and Conflict Studies certificate is worth understanding as its own track, not just a complement to the major. This is one of the oldest and most serious undergraduate programs in the field in the country, built around the premise that understanding political violence, negotiation, and post-conflict reconstruction requires frameworks that transcend any single discipline. Students in this track end up doing their senior honors work on problems that faculty at peer institutions are still publishing papers about.

The Lang Center for Civic and Social Responsibility is the operational nerve center for students who want to connect coursework to real work. Fellowships, community partnerships, and a culture that treats civic engagement as intellectually serious rather than extracurricular mean that the line between what you study and what you do is unusually thin at Swarthmore.

Philadelphia is twenty minutes away by train, and the students who use it well come back with something the seminar room doesn't give: time inside actual policy organizations, city government offices, and advocacy shops. That proximity is an advantage that shows up on transcripts and in recommendation letters.

Two courses that poli sci students consistently flag: the introductory seminar on comparative politics, which is where students discover that the questions are harder and more interesting than the answers, and the senior honors seminar, which functions less like a class and more like a research defense that lasts a full year.

The advisor's last line is usually the same one: arrive with opinions and leave with arguments. The students who thrive here are the ones who are willing to have their prior convictions stress-tested in public, repeatedly, by people who read more than they do.

OUTPUT FORMAT

Write two sections in sequence.

Section 1: The main Academic Program body. Use EXACTLY this heading format: "[Program Name] at [School Name]" (e.g., "Physics at Case Western" or "Political Science at Swarthmore College"). Write the heading on its own line. Then write the body immediately. 150-220 words.

Section 2: The More expansion. Use EXACTLY this heading on its own line: "More: Going Deeper". Then write the body. 200-300 words.

No introductions, no meta-commentary, no additional headings.
